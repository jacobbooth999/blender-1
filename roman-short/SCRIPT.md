## Facts (with sources)
- Legionaries after the Marian reforms (~107 BC) carried their own kit, roughly 30 kg incl. an entrenching tool;
  nicknamed "Marius's mules" (muli Mariani). — Plutarch, *Life of Marius* 13; Frontinus, *Strategemata* 4.1.7.
- On campaign the army dug a ditch and rampart and built a fortified marching camp at the end of each day's march.
  — Polybius, *Histories* 6.27-42; Josephus, *Jewish War* 3.5.
- Roman roads were built in layers (stone foundation, rubble/gravel, sand/concrete, paving); the empire had
  ~80,000 km of paved main roads. — Wikipedia "Roman roads" (citing Vitruvius, *De Architectura* 7.1 for layering).
- 55 BC: Caesar's legions built a timber bridge across the Rhine in 10 days, crossed, stayed 18 days on the far
  side, then came back and destroyed the bridge. — Caesar, *De Bello Gallico* 4.17-19.
- Conquered peoples became allies, then citizens; provincials served in the army (auxiliaries got citizenship on
  discharge). 212 AD, Caracalla's *Constitutio Antoniniana* gave citizenship to almost all free people in the
  empire. — Wikipedia "Constitutio Antoniniana"; "Auxilia".
- Peak extent under Trajan, 117 AD: from northern Britain to Mesopotamia; population est. 50-70 million.
  — Wikipedia "Roman Empire" / Scheidel.
- Augustus set up the Golden Milestone (Milliarium Aureum) in the Forum, 20 BC; the symbolic start of the roads.
  — Wikipedia "Milliarium Aureum".
- 410 AD: Alaric's Visigoths sacked Rome, entering by the Salarian Gate, the gate of the Via Salaria road.
  — Wikipedia "Sack of Rome (410)".

## Script (~330 words ≈ 90 s)
Rome conquered half the known world, and its secret weapon wasn't the sword. It was the shovel.

It's around one hundred BC. Rome is a city of farmers on seven hills, surrounded by neighbours who would really like to burn it down.

So every Roman soldier carries his own gear on his back. Armour, food, a cooking pot and a shovel. Thirty kilos of it. People called them Marius's mules.

At the end of every day's march, they don't just pitch tents. They dig a ditch, pile up a wall, and build a whole fortified camp. Every single night.

Behind the army come the roads. Dead straight, layer on layer of stone, gravel and sand. Eventually more than eighty thousand kilometres of them.

An army that once took a month to arrive now shows up in a week.

Then, in fifty five BC, Julius Caesar wants to cross the Rhine to scare the tribes on the other side. There are no boats. So his men build a wooden bridge across the whole river. In ten days.

Luckily, the tribes were very impressed.
Unluckily for the bridge, Caesar walked back eighteen days later and had it torn down.

Here's the real trick. An empire works like a pizza chain. Winning a town is opening a new branch. Keeping it means the delivery has to arrive hot. Roads were Rome's delivery network. Soldiers, letters, taxes and grain, all moving fast.

And Rome didn't keep the people it beat as enemies. It slowly made them Roman. Their sons joined the legions, picked up shovels, and built the next road. By two twelve AD, almost every free person in the empire was a citizen.

At its peak the empire ran from Scotland to Iraq, with around sixty million people. And in the middle of Rome stood a golden milestone, where every road began.

All roads really did lead to Rome. Which was great, right up until four ten AD, when an army of Goths followed one straight to the gate.
