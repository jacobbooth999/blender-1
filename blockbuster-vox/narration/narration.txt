In October nineteen eighty five a new kind of video store opened its doors in Dallas Texas. It was bright. It was huge. It carried thousands of tapes when most rental shops had only a few hundred. The store was called Blockbuster. Within two decades the name would become one of the most recognised brands in American retail. And then it would almost completely disappear.

To understand how that happened we have to go back to the man who built the empire. In nineteen eighty seven businessman Wayne Huizenga and his partners invested in the young company and took control. He had already built a national waste collection business. He knew how to grow fast. Under his leadership Blockbuster opened stores at a pace the industry had never seen.

The formula was simple. Big stores. Wide selection. Long opening hours. Friday night became a ritual for millions of families. You walked the aisles. You argued over what to watch.

In nineteen ninety four the media giant Viacom bought Blockbuster in a deal reported at around eight point four billion dollars. By the early two thousands the chain had grown to roughly nine thousand stores around the world.

But the business had a hidden engine. Late fees. Customers who returned a tape or a DVD late paid extra. According to widely cited reports Blockbuster earned hundreds of millions of dollars a year from these penalties. Some estimates put the figure near eight hundred million dollars in the year two thousand. The fees were profitable. They were also deeply unpopular.

In nineteen ninety seven two entrepreneurs in California started a small company called Netflix. Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph wanted to rent DVDs through the mail. Their early service struggled. Then they moved to a monthly subscription. No due dates. No late fees.

What happened next became one of the most famous meetings in business history. In the year two thousand the Netflix founders flew to Dallas to meet Blockbuster's leadership. According to Marc Randolph's own account they proposed selling Netflix to Blockbuster for fifty million dollars. Blockbuster's chief executive John Antioco reportedly turned them down. At the time Netflix was losing money. The offer looked like a small company asking for a rescue.

But the story does not end there. Blockbuster did eventually take the threat seriously. In two thousand four it launched its own online rental service. In two thousand five it announced the end of late fees on many rentals. That move reportedly cost the company a large share of its revenue.

Then in two thousand six came Total Access. Customers could rent DVDs by mail and return them in a store for a free rental. Reports from the time suggested the plan was winning subscribers quickly. For a moment Blockbuster looked like it could fight back.

And this is where it gets genuinely strange. The program was expensive. Antioco clashed with his board and with activist investor Carl Icahn over costs and his own pay. In two thousand seven Antioco left the company. His replacement Jim Keyes shifted focus back toward the physical stores. Spending on the online push was cut.

That same year Netflix began streaming films over the internet. At the same time Redbox kiosks were appearing outside supermarkets. They rented DVDs for about a dollar a night. Blockbuster was now being squeezed from two sides.

Then the two thousand eight financial crisis hit. Consumer spending dropped. The company was carrying heavy debt. Stores began closing by the hundreds.

In September twenty ten Blockbuster filed for Chapter Eleven bankruptcy protection. The following year satellite television company Dish Network bought what remained at auction for roughly three hundred twenty million dollars. Dish tried to keep part of the brand alive. By early twenty fourteen the last company owned stores had closed.

A handful of franchise locations kept going on their own. One by one they shut down too. Today according to the latest reports just one Blockbuster store remains open. It sits in the small city of Bend Oregon.

Netflix meanwhile grew into one of the largest entertainment companies on the planet. The company Blockbuster once declined to buy for fifty million dollars went on to be valued at many billions.

So what really brought Blockbuster down? It was not a lack of warning. The company saw online rental coming. It even built a strong answer. The harder problem was letting go of the model that had made it rich. Stores. Shelves. Late fees.

Blockbuster did not lose because it never saw the future. It lost because the future paid less than the past.
